Over a multi-year horizon the chart mostly drops out (that's the lens for this answer only, the short-term read above hasn't changed), and the question becomes whether growth has to do more work than it has been doing to justify the multiple.

The business is a very good one that has compounded slowly. Revenue went from **$394B** in FY22 to **$416B** in FY25, about 5.5% in total over three years, while operating income rose roughly 11% and diluted EPS went from **$6.11** to **$7.46**, up 22% [[src company]]. The gap between revenue growth and EPS growth is margin (operating margin is now 32%) and share count shrinking through buybacks. Free cash flow has gone the other way, from **$111B** in FY22 to **$98.8B** in FY25, so cash generation hasn't kept pace with reported earnings. The recent turn is the counterweight: the June quarter's revenue is up about 16% on the year, against 6.4% for the last fiscal year as a whole.

Against that, the price. At **$340.97** the stock trades on **39.1x** trailing and **35.6x** forward earnings, with a PEG of **2.69** [[src quote]]. FCF yield on the current **$4.98T** market cap works out to roughly 2%. I can't give you a clean comparison with its own multiple history from what the terminal gave me, so I won't claim it's rich versus its own past. The point stands on its own: a buyer at this price is paying for growth well above the 1% to 2% annual revenue pace of FY22 to FY25, and the recent 16% quarter has to prove durable for that to work. Long holds from high multiples have usually been decided by whether earnings growth outran the multiple's compression, and that's the bet being made.

What would make the long-term read wrong in either direction is the next two or three prints. If the June quarter turns out to be a one-off, the multiple looks stretched. If it sustains, 35x forward starts to look more reasonable.
