Because the answer depends on which forecast you use, and the data has two that point opposite ways. Picking one would be choosing the answer for you.

**What happened.** JPMorgan reported earnings per share (EPS, its profit divided by its share count) of **$5.75**, and its shares fell 0.86% [[src desk_section]]. The consensus is the average forecast from analysts, and the feed carries two of them: **$5.715** and **$5.94** [[src desk_section]].

**Did it beat?** Against $5.715 it is $0.035 ahead, about 0.6%. Against $5.94 it is $0.19 short, and Yahoo's earnings history prints that as **-3.24%** [[src research]]. Think of two teachers setting different pass marks: the same score passes one test and fails the other. Why it matters if you hold it: a "beat" label on its own can mislead you, and a 0.6% edge is tiny anyway.

**Why not just pick?** Nothing in the data says which forecast is the right bar, or why they differ. Revenue against its estimate isn't in the free feed either, so there is no second measure to break the tie. Saying "beat" or "miss" would sound firmer than the evidence is.

**What the share price says.** It fell 0.86% on a day when the S&P 500 fund (SPY) closed 0.01% lower [[src desk_section]]. That hints the market wasn't thrilled, but a stock can fall after a beat, and the data shows the move, not the cause. Why it matters: how the bank talks about the future often moves the shares more than the EPS figure, and that commentary isn't in Optic's data.

My honest read: it is a draw, with no clear edge in either direction. CPI tomorrow at 8:30 AM ET is the Desk's open question.
